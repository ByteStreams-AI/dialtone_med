# Plan on 750 calls per physician monthly

A US primary-care practice receives roughly **30 to 50 inbound main-line calls per physician per working day, about 650 to 1,050 a month** [derived from the one US phone-log study, low-to-medium confidence], and the planning number DialTone.Med should use is **750 answered calls per physician per month**. The deck's "~53 inbound calls per physician per day" is not an MGMA figure: it traces to a 2017 vendor blog that added 31 inbound and 22 outbound calls per provider, and the MGMA label first appears, unlinked, on a 2026 vendor blog. Corrected for that error the contradiction shrinks but does not disappear. At 750 per physician, each 750-call pricing band equals one physician, so the published bands cover solo to four-physician practices and every practice of five or more physicians lands in "custom quote", which is the upper half of the stated 1-10 physician size range. The calculator's 500-call default describes only a low-volume solo practice. Specialty pharmacies run at about **0.9 to 1.3 inbound calls per prescription** [derived from URAC audited totals, medium confidence], which puts roughly two-thirds of URAC-accredited organisations inside the bands and most health-system pharmacies above them. Of the missed-call statistics in circulation, only the URAC figures, CallRail's 32% and MGMA's survey result have a traceable source; the 23%, 62% and "7,000-call, 42%" figures do not, and two of them are live in the pricing calculator today. No published study measures total inbound calls at independent 1-10 physician practices, so every per-practice number below is a derivation that the first pilot's call logs should replace.

Labels used throughout: **[measured]** means counted from phone-system or EHR logs, **[survey]** means self-reported, **[derived]** means arithmetic on cited figures, **[vendor claim]** means vendor or trade content with no traceable method, and **[estimate]** marks one unmeasured figure from a journal review. "Unverified" marks anything the researchers saw only in secondary coverage or a search snippet.

## A 2017 vendor calculation, not MGMA, produced "53 calls"

The earliest source for 53 is a Spruce Health blog post dated November 2017, which states "A typical provider has 53 patient calls per day" and never mentions MGMA. The post builds the number from a table of seven practices averaging **21 incoming patient calls per day per 1,000 panel patients** (range 11 to 33) [vendor claim], an assumed panel of 1,500 patients per provider, and a 10:7 inbound-to-outbound ratio from one unnamed study. That yields **31 inbound plus 22 outbound = 53** [vendor claim, derived by the blog's author]. Its citations are broken: two reference numbers are missing from the reference list, one is mislabelled, and the source for the 10:7 ratio is absent ([Spruce Health](https://sprucehealth.com/blog/doctors-losing-hour-day-phone-call-overhead/)). The researchers read the page as updated in August 2026 and did not check an archive copy of the 2017 original.

The MGMA attribution appears nine years later. An AgentZap blog published in January 2026 says "Primary care practices receive approximately 53 inbound calls per physician per day" and gives the source as plain text, "MGMA DataDive, 2025", with no link [vendor claim] ([AgentZap](https://agentzap.ai/blog/medical-practice-phone-statistics)). A RingRx post from May 2026 repeats the sentence with no source at all ([RingRx](https://ringrx.com/?p=17162)). Between 2017 and 2026 three things changed silently: "provider" became "physician", "inbound plus outbound" became "inbound", and "our calculation" became "MGMA". A direct link from Spruce to AgentZap is not proven, but the identical value and the absence of any public MGMA figure point that way.

MGMA has published no per-physician call benchmark in any public page, press release, MGMA Stat article or sponsored report the researchers could open. Its 2017 DataDive release reports speed of answer only: 49 seconds for multispecialty practices and 13.5 seconds for surgical specialties [survey] ([MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/press/mgma-releases-industry-benchmarks-for-optimizing-medical-practice-operations-including-findings-that-average-wait-times-decreased-by-five-minutes-in-2016)). The DataDive Practice Operations product lists "call volume" and "call abandonment rate" as fields, but the values sit behind a paywall ([MGMA DataDive](https://mgma.com/datadive/practice-operations)), so a gated figure cannot be ruled out; confirming it takes an MGMA member login. The one public MGMA volume number is a per-practice survey bucket from a report sponsored by an AI phone-agent vendor: **59% of 302 practice leaders report 301 or more inbound calls per business day**, 54% among independent practices and 77% among hospital- and system-owned groups [survey, vendor-sponsored] ([MGMA/Insight Health](https://www.mgma.com/getkaiasset/3d85ddee-e329-4280-b726-3a93d63c7771/InsightHealth-MGMA-Agentic-Impact-for-Intake-and-Beyond-Final.pdf)). Respondent practice size is not published, so that figure cannot be converted to a per-physician rate.

The company's own buyer-universe document compounds the error. It pairs 53 with "150 to 300 phone calls per week per doctor" and credits both to MGMA. The second figure is an unmeasured estimate from a 2000 review article by Elnicki and colleagues, quoted by the American College of Physicians in 2003 as "an estimated 150 to 300 calls per week, of which about half deal with clinical issues" [estimate] ([ACP](https://www.acponline.org/sites/default/files/acp-policy-library/policies/ambulatory_medicine_reimburse_telephone_care_2003.pdf)). The same document's table of 50-60 calls a day for a solo practice, 150-180 for three physicians and 250-300 for five is 53 rounded and multiplied by headcount; no source measures volume by practice size that way.

## Phone logs show 30 to 50 main-line calls per physician per working day

The literature measures three different things that vendors routinely blur: all inbound calls to the practice's main line, calls that end up as a message or encounter for the physician, and self-reported or marketing figures. A product that answers every inbound call is sized by the first. Most recent large-sample evidence measures the second, which is several times smaller.

### All main-line inbound calls: one study, 439 to 1,002 per 1,000 patients per month

The only US peer-reviewed measurement of total main-line volume per patient is Dexter and colleagues' study of four Oregon primary-care clinics affiliated with a university hospital (community, university, rural and FQHC sites), February 2009 to June 2014. Phone-system logs recorded **439 to 1,002 incoming business-hours calls per 1,000 patients per month** [measured], counting answered and abandoned calls from all caller types and excluding direct lines and after-hours calls ([JABFM 2016](https://www.jabfm.org/content/29/5/613)). The median of the eight published clinic values is 610 per 1,000 per month, which is 29 per 1,000 patients per working day, with a range of 21 to 48 [derived, 21 working days]. That is 5.3 to 12.0 calls per patient per year [derived]. The clinics logged about 3,800 to 11,900 main-line calls a month each [derived, rate times panel] on 38,000 to 56,000 visits a year, or roughly **1.2 to 2.5 inbound calls per office visit** [derived].

Dexter does not report clinician counts, so calls per physician must be derived two ways. Applying the per-1,000 rate to a 1,500 to 2,500 patient panel gives 31 to 119 calls per physician per working day [derived]. Applying calls per visit to 18 to 20 visits a day gives 22 to 50 [derived]; the 18.1 visits per physician per day comes from Baron's 2010 single-practice study [measured, unverified, read through secondary coverage] ([Washington Post](http://www.washingtonpost.com/wp-dyn/content/article/2010/04/28/AR2010042805825.html)). The panel method overstates because Dexter's teaching and safety-net clinics carry large three-year panels relative to their visit volume. The overlap of the two methods, **30 to 50 per physician per working day or 650 to 1,050 per month** [derived], is the most defensible central band. Confidence is low to medium: one study, academic-affiliated clinics, data ending in 2014, before online scheduling and two-way texting.

Two unmeasured figures corroborate the band. Spruce's own inbound component, 31 patient calls per provider per day [vendor claim], sits at its bottom edge, and the Elnicki estimate of 150 to 300 a week, which is 30 to 60 a day [estimate], brackets it. The deck's 53 sits just above the top. A trade-press figure of "100 to 200 calls per physician per day" [vendor claim] ([Physicians Practice](https://www.physicianspractice.com/view/unclog-telephone-bottlenecks)) exceeds everything derivable from measured data and should not be used.

### Calls routed to the physician are a small subset: 4 to 27 a day

EHR studies count only calls that become a message or task for the physician. Across 419 physicians at 141 Yale New Haven sites, primary-care physicians received **6.5 "patient call" inbox messages per day before COVID and 7.8 during** [measured] ([Nath et al., JAMA Network Open 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8511977/)). National EHR data covering more than 280,700 physicians show about **18 patient calls per week** per primary-care physician at the end of 2019 [measured, unverified, read through an AMA summary] ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/digital-health/phone-calls-stable-patient-portal-messages-keep-piling)). Practices that route more to physicians run higher: 23.7 calls per physician per day at a five-internist Philadelphia practice in 2008 [measured, unverified, secondary coverage] ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2010/04/a_doctors_day_is_filled_with_b.html)), and about 23 phone-originated messages per weekday per FTE at MetroHealth in Ohio, which is 27% of 84 inbox messages [measured total, derived share] ([Margolius et al., JABFM 2020](https://www.jabfm.org/content/jabfp/33/3/460.full.pdf)). The sixfold spread reflects how much staff resolve before the physician sees it. None of these counts the calls a front desk answers, and none should be used to size an answering product.

Trend evidence exists only at this physician-routed level, and it argues against a collapse in phone volume. At Yale, portal messages roughly doubled while calls rose 21%, and the authors concluded that "the increase in electronic messages from patients did not displace patient calls" ([Nath et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8511977/)). At the University of Wisconsin, patient calls fell 10.5% between 2019-20 and 2022-23 while portal advice requests rose 55.5% [measured] ([Arndt et al., Annals of Family Medicine 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38253499/)). Calls per patient are flat to modestly down since 2019. No phone-log study of total inbound volume exists after 2014, and nothing measures the effect of online scheduling or texting on calls.

### Vendor planning tables run two to three times lower

AI-receptionist vendors publish planning assumptions, not data. One cost guide lists 300-600 calls a month for a solo provider, 600-1,200 for two to three providers, 1,200-2,400 for four to six and 2,400-4,000 for eight to twelve [vendor claim], which implies 200 to 400 per provider per month ([letsaskclaire.com](https://www.letsaskclaire.com/healthcare/ai-medical-receptionist-cost)). MedReception's calculator defaults to 200 calls a week, about 866 a month [vendor claim] ([MedReception](https://www.medreception.ai/pricing)), and Solutionreach's worked example uses 75 to 100 calls a day for a single-location practice [vendor claim] ([Solutionreach](https://www.solutionreach.com/blog/how-many-calls-does-a-medical-practice-miss-each-week-and-what-it-costs-you)). These inputs span 100 to about 2,100 calls a month for one small practice and agree only loosely. No vendor, including Weave, RingCentral, Talkdesk, Phreesia, Luma Health, Artera and Relatient, was found to publish a call-log benchmark per location with a stated sample.

### Reasons, timing and duration are thinly evidenced

No US study classifies all inbound primary-care calls by reason from call logs. The MGMA/Insight Health survey ranks scheduling and rescheduling in the top three call reasons for 81% of respondents and first for 40%, followed by clinical questions, referrals, refills and insurance questions [survey] ([MGMA/Insight Health](https://www.mgma.com/getkaiasset/3d85ddee-e329-4280-b726-3a93d63c7771/InsightHealth-MGMA-Agentic-Impact-for-Intake-and-Beyond-Final.pdf)). A separate MGMA Stat poll of 294 leaders names eligibility and prior authorization as the most time-consuming phone task for 45%, scheduling for 31% and refills for 6% [survey]; that is a ranking of staff time, not a share of calls ([MGMA Stat](https://mgma.com/mgma-stat/phones-are-still-a-backlog-costing-medical-practices-time)). The only quantified all-calls split comes from Kaiser Permanente Colorado's call center around 2009, where medical advice was 20% of calls and appointments, pharmacy requests and general information made up the rest [measured, unverified, search excerpt] ([Permanente Journal](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2912077)). The defensible statement is qualitative: scheduling leads by count, refills and results are the most common preventable calls ([Deeds et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29667895/)), and payer work leads by staff time. No sourced percentage exists for billing, prior-authorization or referral calls.

After-hours share has no single-dataset measurement either. Splicing a 2000-01 Denver residency practice's after-hours log (4,949 answering-service calls in a year [measured]) ([JABFM 2006](https://www.jabfm.org/content/19/5/437)) against Dexter's business-hours rates gives **4% to 9% of calls arriving after hours** [derived, order of magnitude only, two practices a decade apart]. One answering-service vendor reports 10.6% from "29 million calls" [vendor claim] ([CallMyDoc](https://blog.callmydoc.com/new-blog-1/patient-phone-communication-2026)); the "41% outside 8 to 5" and "38% in the first and last hours" figures in the buyer-universe document trace to unlinked "Relatient" and "Luma Health" attributions on the same AgentZap page that carries 53 [vendor claim] ([AgentZap](https://agentzap.ai/blog/medical-practice-phone-statistics)). Plan on roughly 5% to 10% by clock time [derived, low confidence] and expect a larger share if lunch and daytime overflow are counted.

No measured average call duration exists for total inbound practice calls. Vendors assume 70 seconds ([MedReception](https://www.medreception.ai/pricing)) to 3 minutes ([receptionistcost.com](https://receptionistcost.com/industries/medical/)) [vendor claims]. The only measured durations are physician-handled calls from decades ago: 1.6 minutes in a 1988 rural practice ([Spencer and Daugird](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3404104/)) and 3.4 minutes for seven internists in 1990 ([Johnson and Johnson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2341924/)) [measured, dated]. The calculator's 4 staff minutes per answered call is therefore an assumption with no benchmark behind it, and the page should say so.

## Specialty pharmacies take about one inbound call per prescription

No accreditor, journal, analyst or vendor publishes inbound calls per specialty pharmacy location, per patient or per FTE. URAC supplies the only real volume data. Its mandatory Call Center Performance measure (DTM2010-04) has two parts, the share of business-hours calls answered by a live voice within 30 seconds and the share abandoned before a live answer, and the submissions are externally validated ([URAC 2025 report](https://www.urac.org/sppasr25)). URAC's readiness checklist sets the thresholds at **5% or less abandonment and 80% of calls within 30 seconds** ([URAC](https://www.urac.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Are-You-Readdy-URAC-SPP-5.0.pdf)). ACHC requires "tracking logs for communication/call abandonment and response time frames" under standard DRX2-10F ([ACHC](https://cc.achc.org/Admin/ACHCDocument?Docnum=715)), but no numeric ACHC threshold and no ACHC aggregate results were found.

| 2024 (2023) | 422 | 46,897,711 | 53,004,703 | 84.48% | 2.76% | 1.13 |
| 2025 (2024), published January 2026 | 465 | 58,396,793 | 53,383,561 | 83.15% | 2.98% | 0.91 |

All columns except the last are [measured, accreditor-audited] from the [2023](https://www.urac.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/URAC__Specialty-Pharmacy_Aggregate-Summary-Report_2023_FINAL.pdf), [2024](https://2297879.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na1.net/hubfs/2297879/URAC_Specialty%20Pharmacy_Aggregate%20Summary%20Report_2024.pdf) and [2025](https://www.urac.org/sppasr25) reports. The ratio of **0.9 to 1.3 calls per specialty prescription, averaging 1.1** carries medium confidence. It is volume-weighted toward national PBM-owned and chain pharmacies, it includes non-specialty calls at the 57% of reporters that are not specialty-only, it excludes after-hours calls, and it fell in 2024 because prescriptions rose 24% while calls stayed flat. The mean of about 9,900 calls a month per organisation [derived] is dominated by a handful of giants and describes no typical pharmacy.

Per-location volume follows from the ratio and an assumed 8 to 12 fills per active patient per year; Vanderbilt's specialty pharmacy implies about 9.7 [derived] ([JMCP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10398139)). The fills range is the researchers' assumption, and the scenarios carry low-to-medium confidence.

At the central ratio the 750-call band equals about 820 active patients or 8,200 prescriptions a year, and the 3,000-call ceiling equals about 3,270 patients or 32,700 prescriptions a year. URAC's size distribution shows 56% of accredited organisations dispensing under 16,000 specialty prescriptions a year and a further 9% under 25,000 [measured] ([URAC 2025 report](https://www.urac.org/sppasr25)), so **roughly 65% to 70% of URAC-accredited organisations fit the published bands** [derived]. Health-system pharmacies skew larger: in ASHP's 2024 survey, 56% of 64 respondents fill more than 30,000 specialty prescriptions a year [survey; 67% academic medical centers] ([ASHP via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13747721)), and a site at 60,000 to 75,000 prescriptions implies 4,500 to 8,100 calls a month [derived]. Drug Channels counts **1,910 accredited locations** at end-2025, about 29% independent and 28% hospital or health system ([Drug Channels](https://www.drugchannels.net/2026/05/specialty-pharmacy-accreditation-dcis.html)), so the bands suit independents and smaller provider-owned pharmacies and send most surveyed health-system pharmacies to a quote.

Two points shape positioning. Call-reason shares do not exist for this segment; the only numbers are one vendor's estimate that 60% to 85% of its calls are routine [vendor claim] ([Valeris](https://www.valeris.com/resources/building-the-use-case-the-potential-for-ai-assistants-to-shorten-pharmacy-call-times/)), which describes a non-commercial dispensing model and should not be quoted as an industry figure. And the URAC measure counts answers by a **live voice**. The reports do not say whether a call resolved by an AI agent counts, so no accreditation-benefit claim is safe until the measure specification is checked. The practical upside is that every URAC-accredited prospect already produces monthly telephone reports with total call counts, so actual volume can be requested at qualification.

The URAC pair is accurate but one report behind. The current figures are **2.98% abandonment and 83.15% answered live within 30 seconds** for measurement year 2024 ([URAC 2025 report](https://www.urac.org/sppasr25)); the 30-second rate has fallen three years running. The pricing calculator's pharmacy hint says URAC reported 2.76% "in 2024", which should read "2024 report, 2023 measurement year" or move to 2.98%. More than 10% of accredited pharmacies exceed the 5% abandonment threshold, since the 10th-percentile rate is 5.89% [measured], and about 1.59 million calls to accredited pharmacies were abandoned in 2024 [measured]. Those are stronger sales facts than any missed-call percentage.

For medical practices the measured evidence comes only from large operations with queueing phone systems. Before improvement projects, abandonment ran 8.7% at Emory's imaging queues [measured] ([JMIR Medical Informatics](https://medinform.jmir.org/2026/1/e88441)), 13% at UK HealthCare [measured, unverified, trade newsletter] ([Clinician.com](https://www.clinician.com/articles/63424-abandoned-calls-cut-from-13-to-under-4)) and 34% at a 16-provider Saint Louis University clinic [measured, trade case report] ([HFMA](https://www.hfma.org/revenue-cycle/patient-access/62396/)); afterwards each fell to 5% or lower. The VHA standard is under 5% abandonment and a 30-second average speed of answer ([JGIM 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8806007/)). No measured unanswered or voicemail rate exists for independent small practices, largely because a practice with a few lines and no call distributor cannot measure one. The MGMA result cuts against the vendor numbers: if 36% of leaders report 11% or more at peak, 64% believe they miss less than that even at their worst hour. The likely explanations are that leaders without logs underestimate and that vendor datasets over-sample practices with a problem or count voicemail and after-hours calls as missed. The true rate for small practices is unknown.

This matters for the site today. `public/pricing.html` defaults the general-practice missed-call slider to 23% and calls it "a conservative planning benchmark for medical practices", and hard-codes 62% as the share of unanswered callers who leave no voicemail. Neither has a traceable source. A default of 10% to 15%, presented as a planning assumption bracketed by the MGMA survey and CallRail's 32%, is this report's recommendation, not a measured rate; the 62% constant should become an editable assumption or carry no claim of being a benchmark.

## Bands of 750 cover one to four physicians, then run out

### Planning numbers by practice size

The recommended planning rate is **750 answered calls per physician per month, about 36 per working day** [derived], with a low of 450 and a high of 1,050. The central value is the midpoint of the visit-based range and sits below the 850 midpoint of the overlap band, which allows for the modest decline in calls since Dexter's data. The low is the visit-based floor (1.2 calls per visit, 18 visits a day). A stress case of 1,500 per physician corresponds to Dexter's highest clinic rate on a 1,500-patient panel. Advanced practice clinicians who carry their own panels count as physicians for this purpose. Scaling by headcount is linear by assumption; nothing measures economies of scale.

| Practice size, per location per month | Low (450 each) | Planning (750 each) | High (1,050 each) | Confidence | Band at planning rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Solo | 450 | 750 | 1,050 | Low to medium | Ceiling of band 1; band 2 in a busy quarter |
| 2-5 physicians | 900-2,250 | 1,500-3,750 | 2,100-5,250 | Low | Bands 2, 3 and 4 for two, three and four physicians; five exceeds the top band |
| 6-10 physicians | 2,700-4,500 | 4,500-7,500 | 6,300-10,500 | Low on the level; medium that it exceeds 3,000 | Custom quote throughout |

All values are [derived]. The 6-10 row has the one direct check available: Dexter's clinics, at an estimated 10 to 14 clinician FTE [derived from visit volume], logged 3,800 to 11,900 main-line calls a month [derived from measured rates], which contains the 7,500 planning figure for ten physicians. The MGMA survey finding that 54% of independent respondents report 301 or more calls a day, 6,300 or more a month, confirms that larger independents sit far above 3,000.

Translating logged inbound calls into billable "answered calls" involves offsetting adjustments, none measured. Dexter's counts include abandoned calls and the repeat attempts they cause, which an agent that answers immediately removes; they exclude after-hours calls (add roughly 5% to 10%) and direct-line calls. Hang-ups before a caller reply are not billable. These adjustments are small against the width of the range and their net direction is unknown.

### The bands fit small practices and stop partway through the target range

The corrected arithmetic resolves most of the contradiction. At 53 inbound calls a day a physician generates about 1,100 calls a month and a three-physician practice breaks the 3,000 ceiling; at the planning rate the ceiling holds through four physicians, and at the low rate through six. The 750-call band is, in effect, a per-physician band, which makes the structure easy to explain: one band per physician. The first two bands also line up with the specialty-pharmacy size distribution, and the per-call price of $1.33 falling to $1.23 sits inside the $0.90 to $4 range of published per-call equivalents for medical-capable products [derived from vendor price lists] ([MedReception](https://www.medreception.ai/pricing); [letsaskclaire.com](https://www.letsaskclaire.com/healthcare/ai-medical-receptionist-cost)).

The top band falls short in three places. Every five-physician practice exceeds it at the planning rate, and every three-physician practice does at the high rate. The whole 6-10 physician segment sits at 4,500 to 7,500 calls. Specialty pharmacies above about 3,300 active patients land there too. The research found no count of practices by physician headcount, so the share of target practices affected is unknown, but a pricing page whose stated audience is 1-10 physician practices should not send the upper half of that size range to "contact us". The page already states the rule, "each band of 750 calls adds $900", so publishing bands through **7,500 answered calls ($9,099 a month on the existing step)** discloses nothing new and covers a ten-physician practice at the planning rate; custom quotes then start where the evidence runs out. Whether to taper the step above 3,000 is a commercial choice the evidence does not settle. One vendor guide puts 8-12 provider groups at $7,200 to $12,000 a month [vendor claim] ([letsaskclaire.com](https://www.letsaskclaire.com/healthcare/ai-medical-receptionist-cost)), so a linear extension is not out of market. A second consequence deserves attention: at exactly 750 per physician, a typical practice sits on a band boundary. The three-month averaging rule softens this, but sales conversations should set the expectation that a solo practice will run in band 1 or band 2, not safely inside band 1.

### Calculator default and slider

The 500-call default equals 24 calls a working day, which matches only the low end of a solo practice and the vendor planning tables' solo tier. For general practice the default should be **750, described as "about 750 per physician per month"**, and ideally the control should ask for physician count and prefill calls at 750 each. The slider should run from 100 to at least 7,500 so that a 6-10 physician practice can see itself, with values above the top published band returning "custom quote". For specialty pharmacy, 500 is a sound default: it matches a 500-patient pharmacy at the central ratio. The helper text for both should say the default is a planning estimate and invite the practice's own phone-carrier count.

### What the investor deck should say

The line "Each physician fields ~53 inbound calls a day" in `developer/create-investor-deck.js` (line 336) and its source note "Call volume: MGMA" (line 337) should both go, along with the 53, the 150-300 per week, the headcount table, the 38% and the 41% in `docs/dialtonemed_buyer_universe.md`. A replacement that survives diligence: "Primary-care phone logs show 440 to 1,000 inbound calls per 1,000 patients a month (Dexter et al., JABFM 2016, four clinics). We plan on about 750 answered calls per physician per month, to be validated in pilot." The source note becomes "Call volume: Dexter et al., JABFM 2016; company estimate." The deck can add, with attribution, that 59% of practice leaders in a 2026 MGMA/Insight Health survey report 301 or more inbound calls a day, labelled as a vendor-sponsored survey of practices of unstated size. For pharmacies the audited URAC totals are stronger than anything on the practice side: about 53 million business-hours calls a year at URAC-accredited specialty pharmacies, 1.59 million of them abandoned, across a base Drug Channels counts at 1,910 accredited locations. The deck's bottom-up market size, built on the first and second bands, is consistent with the planning rate for one- and two-physician practices and understates revenue from larger ones, so the correction does not weaken the market case.

## Conclusion

The contradiction among the three numbers was never mainly a pricing problem. It came from importing a per-physician figure that double-counted outbound calls and carried a borrowed credential. Replace it with a derived 750 and the bands, the calculator and the deck tell one story: one band per physician, published through ten. What remains is a real coverage gap above 3,000 calls, plus two unsourced statistics on the pricing page that carry the same diligence risk as the 53.

The larger finding is that nobody has published what this product will be first to know. No study measures total inbound calls at independent small practices after 2014, by practice size, by reason or by hour, and no source reports calls per specialty pharmacy location at all. Every planning number here has low-to-medium confidence and a short shelf life. A month of carrier call-detail records from each pilot site, plus the URAC telephone reports pharmacy prospects already hold, would replace the derivations with measurements, and would give the company a benchmark the market currently lacks.
